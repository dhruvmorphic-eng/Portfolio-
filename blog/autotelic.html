I am autotelic.

And I didn't know the word for it until recently.

There's a word for the thing I've been doing my entire life without knowing what to call it. The word is autotelic — from the Greek auto (self) and telos (goal, purpose). An autotelic person is someone who does things not because the world told them to, but because the doing itself is the point.

I dropped out of Manipal after one semester. Not because I was failing. Not because I had some grand exit strategy or a term sheet waiting in my inbox. I dropped out because the thing I was building felt more real than anything a classroom could hand me. The act of building was the reward. The degree was supposed to be the reward, and it just... wasn't.

That's the autotelic instinct. You don't need the trophy to justify the race.

The scoreboard nobody sees

When I was younger, I played a cricket match in Hosur. I was unwell — the kind of unwell where people tell you to sit this one out. I took four wickets that day. Got Man of the Match.

Here's the thing people misunderstand about that story: they think the point is the award. The stat line. It's not. The point is that I walked out to play anyway. Not to prove something to the crowd. Not because someone needed me to. I walked out because I couldn't not. I wanted to play. The game itself was pulling me forward.

That's what being autotelic feels like. The scoreboard is for everyone else. You're playing because you love the sound the bat makes when it connects clean.

Why most people burn out (and I don't)

I run a B2B SaaS company. I run a content studio. I make AI-animated films for fun. I study markets and build investment portfolios. I'm 19.

People ask me how I don't burn out. The honest answer is: because I'm not doing any of this for something. I'm doing it as something. There's a difference.

When you work toward an external goal — a funding round, a follower count, a salary — you're essentially borrowing motivation from the future. You're saying: I'll suffer now so that I can feel good later. That's fine. It works. Until it doesn't. Until the round doesn't close, the followers don't come, the salary doesn't feel like enough. Then you crash, because the fuel was never yours. It was rented.

Autotelic motivation doesn't rent. It owns. The work is the fuel. The curiosity is the engine. The thing you're building teaches you something, and that lesson opens a door, and behind the door is another thing to build. It's recursive. It feeds itself.

I don't burn out because I'm not burning anything. I'm generating.

The dropout question

Every founder who left college early gets the same question: "Was it worth it?"

The question assumes there was a trade. That I gave up one thing to get another. A degree for a company. Security for risk. But that framing only works if the degree was the goal. It wasn't. The goal was always to be in motion — to be making things, learning by breaking things, talking to people who are building the future instead of reading about people who already did.

I didn't drop out of something. I dropped into something.

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi — the psychologist who gave us the word autotelic — spent his career studying flow states. That feeling when time disappears and you're completely absorbed in what you're doing. His research showed that autotelic people experience flow more frequently, not because they're special, but because they choose activities for the experience itself rather than the outcome. They structure their own challenges. They find complexity where others see routine.

When I sit down at midnight to figure out how to score buying-intent signals from the open web, nobody's watching. There's no deadline. No investor breathing down my neck. I'm doing it because the problem is beautiful. Because signal detection at scale is a puzzle that rewards you with better questions the deeper you go.

That's flow. That's autotelic. That's the thing.

The myth of the finish line

Here's what nobody tells you about external goals: they move. You hit the revenue target, and now there's a bigger one. You close the round, and now you need to close the next one. You get the thing, and the thing becomes the floor.

Autotelic people don't have this problem — not because they don't set goals, but because the goal was never the point. The goal is a compass direction. It tells you which way to walk. But the walking is the life. The goal is just an excuse to keep walking.

I set aggressive targets. I want Cnvrted to be the signal-intelligence layer that every sales team in the world runs on. I want Banao to be a media empire. I want to eventually run a portfolio management firm. These aren't small ambitions.

But if you took all of that away tomorrow — the product, the team, the traction — I'd start building again on Monday. Not because I'm disciplined. Because I can't help it. Because the building is who I am, not what I do.

A note for the people who recognize this

If you're reading this and something clicks — if you've always felt like the process mattered more than the prize, if you've been called obsessive when you were really just engaged, if you've ever stayed up until 4 AM on something nobody asked you to do and woke up wanting to do it again — you might be autotelic too.

It's not a personality type you develop. It's one you recognize. You've always been this way. You just didn't have the word.

Now you do.

The word is autotelic. And it means the goal lives inside the doing. It means you are both the runner and the finish line. It means the work is not a means to an end — it is the end, endlessly unfolding.

Welcome to the loop. It never stops. That's the whole point.
