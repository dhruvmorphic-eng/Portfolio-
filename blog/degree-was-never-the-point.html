Your degree was never the point.

Every year, millions of eighteen-year-olds sit down and answer a question that's treated like a life sentence: "What are you going to major in?"

We've built an entire system around the idea that this one decision locks in your next forty years. Pick Computer Science, you're coding forever. Pick English, you're teaching or editing manuscripts. Pick something like Philosophy or Art History, and everyone around you starts asking what your backup plan is.

I dropped out after one semester. Not because I didn't respect education — because I realized the degree was a proxy for something I could get faster by just building.

Here's the thing nobody tells you at eighteen: the connection between your major and your career is mostly a myth.

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York has the data to back this up — roughly 27% of college graduates end up working in a field directly related to their major. Flip that number around and it means three out of every four people in any office, agency, or boardroom are doing something completely disconnected from what their diploma says. The system we're all pressured into optimizing for barely predicts the outcome it claims to guarantee.

Why the degree stopped mattering

Employers hire skills and character, not course codes.

Unless you're heading into a strictly licensed field — medicine, civil engineering, law — nobody's actually checking your transcript. What they're checking is whether you can think clearly, communicate under pressure, adapt when the plan falls apart, and execute without someone holding your hand.

A philosophy major who can break down a complex argument and explain it simply will outperform a business major who can't string two coherent sentences together in a client meeting. I've seen this firsthand building teams — the person who studied something "impractical" but learned how to think rigorously is often sharper than the person who memorized a syllabus that maps neatly to their job title.

College was never about the content — it was about learning how to learn.

The actual value of those four years isn't the specific facts in the textbook. It's the discipline of showing up, structuring an argument, managing your own time without a parent enforcing it, and navigating environments full of people who don't think like you. That's the real curriculum. The topic of your term paper is just the vehicle.

I got a version of that compressed into a year of building instead of four years of lectures — different vehicle, same underlying skill: learning how to learn fast, under pressure, with real stakes attached.

The market moves faster than any university can.

Universities are slow by design. Curriculum changes go through committees, approvals, tenure reviews — a process that takes years. Meanwhile the actual economy shifts every few quarters. Growth marketing, prompt engineering, signal intelligence, no-code development — none of this existed in a syllabus when most current professionals were choosing their majors. By the time a specialized textbook gets published, half of it is already outdated. You end up learning the real version on the job regardless of what you studied.

Why so many people walk away from their major

If the major doesn't lock in your future, why do so many graduates actively pivot away from what they spent four years studying? A few patterns keep showing up.

The gap between the classroom and the actual job. Political science looks fascinating when you're debating international relations theory in a seminar. It looks a lot different when your entry-level job is 80 cold calls a day for donor outreach. The academic version and the working version of most fields are barely related. When people realize this, walking away isn't failure — it's clarity.

Money is not optional. Passion for literature or sociology is real. So is rent. So are student loans. When the entry-level pay in your field of study doesn't cover your actual life, pivoting toward higher-paying, higher-growth industries isn't a betrayal of your interests — it's basic math.

You're not the same person you were at eighteen. Nobody has enough life experience at that age to lock in a forty-year career decision. Your twenties expose you to industries, people, and problems you had zero visibility into as a teenager. A biology major discovering they love product design isn't a contradiction. It's just what happens when you actually go out and live.

Burnout is real and preventable. Certain paths — pre-med, corporate law, high-finance tracks — come stapled to brutal hours and toxic cultures. Plenty of people get a taste of that in an internship and consciously choose a different life. That's not weakness. That's someone who did the math on what their twenties and thirties are actually worth.

Skills stack, and once you see it, you can't unsee it. Writing essays becomes copywriting. Running a lab experiment becomes structured product thinking. Organizing a student club becomes running events and managing timelines under pressure. Once you notice that your foundational skills translate everywhere, staying boxed into your declared major stops making sense.

The best industries right now didn't exist when the curriculum was written. Growth, content strategy, AI-native products, signal intelligence — these are fields being built in real time. Nobody teaches them in a four-year program because the field is younger than the students studying it. If you want to work at the edge of what's actually happening, you have to step outside the classroom to find it.

Building something yourself teaches you more than any single major could. Running a company forces you into sales, branding, financial management, and client relationships simultaneously — often in the same week. No undergraduate track prepares you for that kind of range. You build the range by doing the thing, not by studying an abstraction of the thing.

What I'd tell someone staring at this decision

Stop treating your major like it's a contract you can't break.

Your degree — or the absence of one — isn't a life sentence. It's not even the most interesting thing about you. What actually matters is whether you can set a goal, push through the boring parts, synthesize information fast, and finish what you start. You can prove all of that inside a classroom or completely outside one.

A few things I'd actually act on if I were starting over:

Own the narrative instead of apologizing for it. Whatever background you have — finished degree, dropped out, self-taught — frame it as an asset, not a gap you need to explain away. My own path gets brought up in almost every serious conversation I have with investors or collaborators. Nobody's ever penalized me for it. If anything, it's the first thing that makes people lean in.

Build a portfolio, not a transcript. Nobody hands me a job because of a piece of paper. They look at what I've shipped — the company, the content studio, the actual output. Proof of work will always outweigh a line on a resume. If you're not in school, this matters even more. If you are, start building it now instead of waiting for graduation.

Become a permanent self-educator. The people actually winning right now aren't the ones who memorized a textbook once. They're the ones who never stopped updating their skill set — through real projects, real failures, and constantly rebuilding their understanding as the field moves.

Your major, your degree, your dropout story — none of it is the finish line. It's just where you started running from.

Where you go from here is entirely on you.
